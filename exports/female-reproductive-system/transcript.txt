The female reproductive system
Welcome to CBE STEM Academy, Grade Seven Integrated Science. Today we explore the female reproductive system. You will identify its main organs, explain their functions, and follow an egg after ovulation.

Where are these organs?
Most female reproductive organs are inside the pelvis, in the lower part of the abdomen. This front-view cutaway lets us see inside. The organs are shown more clearly than they would appear in a living body.

Meet the main organs
There are two ovaries, one on each side of the uterus. Two fallopian tubes curve above them. The uterus lies near the centre. Its narrow lower part is the cervix, which opens into the vagina. Follow the labels. The vagina is a canal inside the body; the external genital structures are collectively called the vulva.

Ovaries: eggs and hormones
The ovaries are small, oval organs. They contain immature egg cells, each surrounded by supporting cells in a follicle. From puberty, hormones help follicles develop during reproductive cycles. Usually one egg is released during a cycle in which ovulation occurs. The ovaries also produce hormones, including oestrogen and progesterone.

Fallopian tubes: transport
The fallopian tubes provide a route towards the uterus. Finger-like ends called fimbriae lie close to each ovary. Notice the small gap: the tube is not sealed directly onto the ovary. The fimbriae help guide a released egg into the tube.

Uterus: a muscular organ
The uterus is a hollow, muscular organ. Its inner lining is called the endometrium. During a reproductive cycle, this lining can thicken in preparation for a possible pregnancy. If pregnancy becomes established, a developing embryo is supported inside the uterus. Identify the wall and the lining separately.

Cervix: the lower neck
The cervix is the narrow lower part of the uterus. A small cervical canal connects the uterine cavity with the vagina. It produces mucus.

Vagina: a muscular canal
The vagina is a muscular canal extending from the cervix to the outside of the body. Menstrual blood and tissue pass through it. It also forms part of the birth canal. Urine leaves through a different opening, the urethra. The vagina is not the urinary passage.

Ovulation: releasing an egg
Now watch a simplified close-up of ovulation. A follicle grows as the egg inside it develops. When ovulation occurs, the follicle opens and releases the egg from the surface of the ovary. The nearby fimbriae help guide it towards the opening of the fallopian tube. An egg does not jump straight into the uterus. The egg is enlarged here so you can see it. The movement and timing are slowed or compressed for learning.

How does the egg move?
An egg cannot swim along the tube by itself. Tiny hair-like structures called cilia beat along the tube lining. Their coordinated movement, together with gentle muscle contractions, helps carry the egg towards the uterus. Fertilization, when a sperm joins with an egg, usually happens in a fallopian tube. We will explore that process in a separate lesson. If the egg is not fertilized, it eventually breaks down. Movement is enlarged and time is compressed for learning.

The lining changes during the cycle
The ovaries and uterus change together during the menstrual cycle. Hormones help the uterine lining build up. If pregnancy does not occur, hormone changes lead to shedding of the lining. Blood and tissue pass through the cervix and vagina. This is menstruation. Cycle lengths vary, especially during adolescence. Ovulation does not always happen on the same numbered day.

Check your understanding
Pause and think: which organs contain developing egg cells? The ovaries. Which organ has the lining that thickens and may be shed? The uterus. That lining is the endometrium.

Remember the route
How does an egg move along a fallopian tube? Cilia and gentle muscle contractions help move it. Remember: ovaries contain eggs, tubes transport, and the uterus supports pregnancy. Thank you for learning.
