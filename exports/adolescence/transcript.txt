Physical changes during adolescence
Welcome to CBE STEM Academy, Grade Seven Integrated Science. Today we explore physical changes during adolescence. We will compare changes in girls and boys, learn what they share, and practise simple personal care. Every body deserves respect.

What starts puberty?
Adolescence is the time between childhood and adulthood. Puberty is the process in which the body grows and becomes physically mature. Signals from the brain help glands release hormones. Hormones are chemical messengers carried in the blood. They help different parts of the body change. These changes happen gradually, not in one day.

Shared change: a growth spurt
Both girls and boys usually have a growth spurt. This means growing taller more quickly for a time. Bones lengthen, muscles develop, and hands or feet may grow before the rest of the body catches up. Watch the figures grow gradually. The drawings are examples, not a rule for your own height, size, or shape.

Shared changes: hair, sweat and skin
Hair begins to grow in the underarms and around the private parts, called the pubic area. Hair on the arms and legs may become thicker or darker. Sweat glands become more active, so body odour may become stronger. Skin can become oilier and develop spots or acne. These changes do not mean someone is dirty.

Typical physical changes in girls
In girls, breasts begin to develop, and the hips may become wider. Body shape changes gradually as bones grow and body fat is distributed differently. Breast development can begin on one side before the other, and sizes vary. The ovaries, uterus, and other reproductive organs also mature. Our diagrams stay clothed. There is no single correct body shape, and it is unkind to compare or tease others.

Menstruation: understanding periods
During puberty, girls begin having menstrual periods. Inside the uterus, the inner lining builds up. If pregnancy does not happen, some of this lining leaves the body with blood through the vagina. This is menstruation, or a period. It is not a wound or a sign of being unclean. Early periods may not follow a regular pattern. Ask a trusted adult if you are unsure.

Typical physical changes in boys
In boys, the shoulders often become broader and muscles develop. The testes and penis grow, and the testes begin to produce sperm. Facial hair may gradually appear. Sometimes semen is released during sleep. This is called a wet dream. It is a normal body process, not an illness or something to be ashamed of. The amount of facial hair and the timing of these changes differ between boys.

Why can the voice change?
The voice box, called the larynx, grows during puberty. As the vocal cords grow, the voice changes. The change is usually more noticeable in boys, whose voices often become deeper. For a while, the voice may crack or switch pitch. This is common during the change. Nobody should be teased about their voice.

Personal care: simple daily habits
Wash your body regularly, especially after sweating, and wear clean underwear and clothes. Clean your face gently. Scrubbing hard or picking spots can make skin problems worse. Brush your teeth, eat varied meals, drink water, stay active, and get enough sleep. If acne is painful or persistent, ask a trusted adult or health worker for help. Puberty is a good time to build healthy habits.

Managing periods with confidence
For periods, use a suitable clean menstrual product, such as a pad. Change it regularly, follow its instructions, and wash your hands before and after changing it. Carry a spare product if you can. Periods should not stop someone from learning or joining activities. Ask a trusted adult or health worker about severe pain, very heavy bleeding, or other worries.

Different timing is normal
Puberty starts at different ages. Many girls begin between about eight and thirteen years, and many boys between about nine and fourteen. These are broad starting ranges, not dates when all changes must be finished. Even classmates of the same age can be at different stages. Avoid teasing or judging bodies. If changes seem very early, very late, or worrying, speak to a trusted adult or health worker.
