The journey of a meal
Your digestive system breaks food into small pieces your body can take in and use. Enzymes help this breakdown. Follow a meal from mouth to anus. The liver, gallbladder and pancreas add liquids, but food does not pass through them.

1. Ingestion and the mouth
Teeth grind food. Your tongue mixes it with saliva, then forms a soft ball called a bolus. Salivary glands make saliva. It wets food and contains amylase, which starts breaking starch into smaller sugars. Tongue glands make lingual lipase. This enzyme helps digest fat, mainly in the stomach.

2. Swallowing and peristalsis
Your tongue pushes food into the throat. A flap called the epiglottis helps keep food out of the windpipe. Muscles squeeze food down the food pipe, or oesophagus. These squeezing waves are called peristalsis. No new enzymes are added here.

3. The stomach: mixing and protein digestion
The stomach mixes food into a thick liquid called chyme. Acid helps pepsin work. Pepsin cuts proteins into smaller pieces called peptides. Acid is not an enzyme. It stops salivary amylase working. Gastric and lingual lipases digest some fat. Mucus protects the stomach wall. The stomach also makes intrinsic factor, a protein needed later.

4. Support organs supply the duodenum
Food enters the duodenum, the first part of the small intestine. The pancreas adds enzymes for starch, protein and fat. It also adds bicarbonate to make the mixture less acidic. The liver makes bile. The gallbladder stores and releases it. Bile breaks large fat drops into smaller drops. It is not an enzyme.

5. Pancreatic digestion: carbohydrates
Pancreatic amylase continues breaking starch into smaller sugar pieces. Surface enzymes will finish the job. We cannot make cellulase, the enzyme that breaks down cellulose in plant cell walls. This fibre moves towards the colon.

6. Pancreatic digestion: proteins
The pancreas sends protein-digesting enzymes in a switched-off form, helping protect itself. An intestinal enzyme called enteropeptidase switches on trypsin. Trypsin switches on other protein-digesting enzymes. Trypsin, chymotrypsin and elastase cut protein chains. Carboxypeptidase removes amino acids, the building blocks of protein, from the ends.

7. Pancreatic digestion: fats and nucleic acids
Pancreatic lipase breaks fat into fatty acids and smaller fat pieces called monoglycerides. A protein called colipase helps. Phospholipase and cholesterol esterase act on other fats. Bile helps bring fat pieces to the intestinal wall. R N ase and D N ase break down genetic material from food cells.

8. Brush-border enzymes finish digestion
Surface enzymes finish digestion. Maltase changes maltose into glucose, a simple sugar used for energy. Sucrase acts on table sugar. Lactase acts on milk sugar. Glucoamylase and isomaltase finish starch pieces. Trehalase breaks down trehalose. Aminopeptidases and dipeptidases turn protein pieces into amino acids. Nucleotidases, phosphatases and nucleosidases finish breaking down genetic material. The products are small enough for absorption.

9. Zoom into the villi
Absorption means taking digested food through the intestinal wall into the body. Tiny finger-like projections called villi cover this wall. Each villus has a thin covering, tiny blood vessels, and a central lymph tube called a lacteal. Smaller projections called microvilli cover each absorptive cell. They increase the surface area for taking in nutrients.

10. Two routes out of a villus
Simple sugars and amino acids pass into tiny blood vessels. Blood takes them to the liver. Most long-chain fat pieces are packed into tiny bundles called chylomicrons inside intestinal cells. These bundles enter lacteals, travel in lymph, then join the blood. Some shorter fatty acids enter blood directly. Water, vitamins and minerals are also absorbed. Cells use nutrients for energy, growth and repair: assimilation.

11. The ileum has special absorption roles
Much absorption happens in the jejunum. It continues in the ileum. The last part of the ileum absorbs vitamin B twelve with intrinsic factor, the protein made in the stomach. It also recovers bile salts for reuse. Remaining material enters the large intestine.

12. The colon and its helpful bacteria
The colon takes in more water and salts, making the remaining material firmer. Helpful bacteria use their enzymes to break down some fibre. They make small fatty acids, including butyrate, which feeds colon cells. These bacteria make vitamin K and some B vitamins. They help protect the gut, but also produce gas. Undigested food, bacteria, old cells and water form faeces.

13. Rectum, anus and egestion
The rectum stores faeces. Rings of muscle called anal sphincters control release through the anus. This is egestion: passing out undigested and unabsorbed material. Excretion instead removes wastes made by body cells, such as urea. Remember: ingestion, digestion, absorption, assimilation, then egestion.
