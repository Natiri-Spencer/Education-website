The journey of a meal
Digestion breaks large food molecules into small molecules that can be absorbed. Follow a meal from ingestion in the mouth to egestion through the anus. Accessory organs add secretions. Food does not pass through the liver, gallbladder or pancreas.

1. Ingestion and the mouth
Ingestion brings food into the mouth. Teeth grind it, increasing surface area. The tongue mixes food with saliva and forms a bolus. Salivary glands add water, mucus and salivary amylase. Amylase starts starch digestion into maltose and shorter chains. Tongue glands release lingual lipase. This fat-digesting enzyme acts mainly after swallowing, in the stomach.

2. Swallowing and peristalsis
The tongue pushes the bolus into the pharynx. The epiglottis helps protect the airway. Peristalsis, waves of muscle contraction, pushes food down the oesophagus. No digestive enzymes are added here. A sphincter admits food into the stomach and limits backflow.

3. The stomach: mixing and protein digestion
Stomach muscles churn food into chyme. Hydrochloric acid unfolds proteins and activates pepsin from pepsinogen. Pepsin cuts proteins into peptides. Acid is not an enzyme. Increasing acidity inactivates salivary amylase. Gastric and lingual lipases digest some fat. Mucus protects the lining. The stomach also produces intrinsic factor for later vitamin B twelve absorption.

4. Support organs supply the duodenum
Chyme enters the duodenum. The pancreas supplies bicarbonate to neutralise acid, and enzymes to digest carbohydrates, proteins and fats. The liver makes bile. The gallbladder stores, concentrates and releases bile through ducts into the duodenum. Bile salts emulsify large fat droplets into smaller droplets, increasing surface area for lipase. Bile is not an enzyme.

5. Pancreatic digestion: carbohydrates
Pancreatic amylase continues starch digestion, producing maltose, maltotriose and branched fragments called dextrins. Intestinal surface enzymes must finish their breakdown into single sugars. Humans lack cellulase, so cellulose fibre can reach the colon.

6. Pancreatic digestion: proteins
The pancreas releases protein-digesting enzymes as inactive precursors. Intestinal enteropeptidase activates trypsinogen into trypsin. Trypsin activates other pancreatic proteases. Trypsin, chymotrypsin and elastase cut protein chains internally. Carboxypeptidases release amino acids from peptide ends. Intestinal peptidases complete digestion.

7. Pancreatic digestion: fats and nucleic acids
Pancreatic lipase breaks triglycerides mainly into fatty acids and monoglycerides. Colipase assists lipase; it is a helper protein, not a digestive enzyme. Phospholipase digests phospholipids. Cholesterol esterase acts on cholesterol esters. Bile salts carry fat products towards intestinal cells. Pancreatic R N ase and D N ase break dietary R N A and D N A into nucleotides.

8. Brush-border enzymes finish digestion
Brush-border enzymes finish digestion. Maltase and glucoamylase release glucose. Sucrase makes glucose and fructose; lactase makes glucose and galactose. Isomaltase, or alpha dextrinase, splits branched carbohydrate fragments. Trehalase digests trehalose into glucose. Aminopeptidases and dipeptidases finish peptide digestion into amino acids. Phosphatases, nucleotidases and nucleosidases finish nucleotide digestion. These enzymes work on or near intestinal cells.

9. Zoom into the villi
The small-intestinal lining has folds and finger-like villi. Each villus contains blood capillaries and a central lymph vessel called a lacteal. Tiny microvilli on absorptive cells form the brush border. Folds, villi and microvilli greatly increase surface area. The covering is one cell thick. Nutrients are absorbed throughout the small intestine, including the ileum.

10. Two routes out of a villus
Single sugars and amino acids cross intestinal cells using transport proteins. They enter blood capillaries and travel to the liver in portal blood. Most long-chain fat products are rebuilt into triglycerides and packaged as chylomicrons inside intestinal cells. Chylomicrons enter lacteals, travel through lymph, then reach blood. Shorter-chain fatty acids can enter portal blood directly. Water, minerals and vitamins are also absorbed. Cells use nutrients for energy, growth and repair: assimilation.

11. The ileum has special absorption roles
The jejunum absorbs much of the digested food; the ileum continues absorption. The terminal ileum absorbs vitamin B twelve bound to intrinsic factor, and reabsorbs bile salts for return to the liver. Remaining material passes through the ileocaecal valve into the large intestine.

12. The colon and its helpful bacteria
The colon absorbs more water and salts, making contents firmer. It adds no human digestive enzymes, but bacteria supply their own. Bacteria ferment some fibre into short-chain fatty acids: acetate, propionate and butyrate. Butyrate fuels colon-lining cells. Microbes make vitamin K and some B vitamins, and help support the gut barrier and resist harmful microbes. Fermentation also produces gas. Remaining fibre, bacteria, shed cells and water form faeces.

13. Rectum, anus and egestion
The rectum stores faeces. Stretching triggers a defecation reflex. Anal sphincters control release through the anus. This is egestion: removal of undigested and unabsorbed material. Excretion instead removes metabolic wastes, such as urea. Remember: ingestion, digestion, absorption, assimilation, then egestion.
