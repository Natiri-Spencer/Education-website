Implantation and pregnancy
Welcome to CBE STEM Academy, Grade Seven Integrated Science. How does a fertilised egg become a baby? We will follow early cell division, implantation, and growth inside the uterus. Watch the labels and follow each stage.

The journey to the uterus
Fertilisation usually happens in a fallopian tube. The new single cell is called a zygote. It divides into two cells, then four, and then many more. The group of cells moves towards the uterus. Tiny hairs and movements of the tube help carry it. The cells do not swim like sperm.

A blastocyst forms
After several days, a hollow ball of cells forms. This stage is called a blastocyst. Look for its outer cells, fluid-filled space, and small group of inner cells. The inner cells will develop into the embryo. The blastocyst comes out of its protective coat before attaching to the uterus.

Implantation: attaching to the lining
The uterus has a soft, blood-rich inner lining called the endometrium. About six to seven days after fertilisation, the blastocyst begins to attach to this lining. Its outer cells help it settle into the lining. This gradual process is called implantation. It is not a baby sticking to the outside of the uterus. The lining supports early development. Implantation takes several days. The tiny developing embryo stays inside the uterus, where it can continue to grow.

The embryo begins to take shape
After implantation, cells grow and take on different jobs. Some help form the brain and spinal cord. Others form the heart, muscles, and other body parts. Small arm and leg buds appear. The developing human is called an embryo during these early weeks. The organs are beginning to form; they are not fully mature.

Placenta and umbilical cord
A temporary organ called the placenta develops where the pregnancy attaches to the uterine lining. An umbilical cord connects the developing baby to the placenta. Oxygen and nutrients pass from the mother's blood across the placenta to the baby's blood. Carbon dioxide and other wastes pass back in the other direction. The two blood supplies normally stay separate. The cord carries blood between the baby and placenta. The baby does not eat through its mouth inside the uterus.

A protected place to grow
The baby is surrounded by a thin bag called the amniotic sac. Inside it, amniotic fluid cushions the baby and allows movement. The uterus has strong muscular walls. Its lower opening is the cervix. During most of pregnancy, the cervix stays closed. The baby grows inside the uterus, not inside the stomach.

First trimester: the body plan forms
Pregnancy is divided into three parts called trimesters. During the first trimester, the main body structures begin to form. Arms and legs grow, and fingers and toes develop. After about eight weeks from fertilisation, the developing baby is called a fetus. That is about ten weeks on the pregnancy calendar. Its organs still need time to mature.

Second trimester: growth and movement
During the second trimester, the fetus grows larger. Bones and muscles develop further. The arms and legs can move, and the mother may begin to feel movements. The placenta and cord keep supplying oxygen and nutrients. The uterus expands to make room. The fetus is becoming more developed, but it is not yet ready for birth.

Third trimester: preparing for birth
During the third trimester, the fetus gains weight and stores fat. The brain and lungs continue to mature. There is less space as the baby grows, but movement continues. Many babies turn so their head points towards the cervix before birth. Look at the placenta on the uterine wall and the cord leading to the baby's abdomen. This model speeds up months of growth. Real development happens gradually, and the timing varies between pregnancies.

Gestation: how long does pregnancy last?
The time a baby develops in the uterus before birth is called gestation. Human pregnancy usually lasts about forty weeks, or roughly nine months, counted from the first day of the last menstrual period. That is about thirty-eight weeks after fertilisation. These are two ways of counting the same pregnancy. Birth does not always happen on the estimated due date.
