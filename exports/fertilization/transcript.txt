Fertilisation: the sperm's journey
Welcome to CBE STEM Academy, Grade Seven Integrated Science. Today we follow sperm through the human reproductive system. Watch one sperm fertilise an egg, and learn what happens to the others.

Where does fertilisation happen?
Look at this front-view cutaway of the female reproductive organs. Find the vagina, cervix, uterus, and fallopian tubes. An ovary releases an egg during ovulation. The nearby tube helps collect it. Fertilisation usually happens in the wider part of a fallopian tube, not inside the uterus.

Sperm begin the journey
After semen is released into the vagina, sperm may move towards the cervix. Semen is a fluid mixture containing sperm cells. A sperm’s tail helps it swim. Movements of the reproductive tract also help transport sperm. Our markers are enlarged. A few drawn sperm represent the many sperm present.

Through the cervix
The cervix is the narrow lower part of the uterus. Sperm must pass through its mucus to continue the journey. Near ovulation, this mucus can allow sperm to pass more easily. Many sperm remain behind, become trapped, or lose movement. Some semen and sperm leave the vagina with fluid. Only a small proportion continue upwards.

Across the uterus
Sperm that pass the cervix move through the uterus towards the fallopian tubes. They do not enter an ovary. Many never reach an egg. Some take a route where no egg is present. Others lose movement or are cleared by the body’s defence cells. This is not a simple race with a guaranteed winner.

Meeting the egg
Now we zoom into a fallopian tube, where an egg may be waiting. Much fewer sperm reach this place than began the journey. Sperm also undergo changes inside the female reproductive tract that help them become able to fertilise an egg. This preparation is called capacitation. Our view is enlarged so that we can see the cells clearly.

The egg has protective layers
The egg cell is surrounded by a layer of other cells and a clear protective coat. The coat is called the zona pellucida. Inside the coat is the egg's cell membrane. Sperm must get through the outer cells and the coat to reach this membrane. Changes at the sperm's head, including the release of enzymes, help it pass through these layers.

One sperm joins with the egg
Follow the highlighted sperm. Its colour helps us track it; it does not mean this sperm is stronger or better. It reaches the egg’s membrane. The sperm and egg membranes join. The sperm enters, bringing its genetic information inside. This joining is part of fertilisation. Normally, only one sperm fertilises one egg. The tail also enters and is later broken down. Watch the close-up slowly.

The egg blocks further entry
Sperm entry starts changes inside the egg. Tiny packets release substances that change the protective coat. The egg’s membrane also becomes less able to join with another sperm. These changes help prevent extra sperm from entering. The others stay outside. They are not pushed away by electricity, and do not all die at the same instant.

What happens to the other sperm?
Most sperm never reach the egg. Some are lost with fluid, some are trapped, and some are cleared by defence cells. Sperm that remain outside the fertilised egg cannot join it. Over time, unused sperm lose movement and break down. The body clears the remains. This happens over time, not all at once. Only the sperm that joined the egg contributes its genetic information to this new cell.

A new cell: the zygote
The fertilised egg is called a zygote. It contains genetic information from the egg and the sperm, which helps determine inherited features. The zygote begins dividing while travelling along the tube towards the uterus. We show two cells, then four. The early embryo later reaches the uterus. Implantation is a different process, for our next lesson.
